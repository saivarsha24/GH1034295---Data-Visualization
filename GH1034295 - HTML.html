Explanatory Data Analysis: Customer and Sales Insights for a UK-Based Online Gift Retailer¶

*Dataset Source: https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/vijayuv/onlineretail?select=OnlineRetail.csv*

1. Introduction and Business Context¶

1.1 Role and Objective¶

This project adopts the role of a data science consultant. Our client is a UK-based purely online retailer having no presence of brick and mortar stores. Gift items are their main offerings that are entirely sold via their website. The biggest part of the clients consists of small wholesalers who do bulk orders and not small consumers who buy single items. The key task was building an EDA pipeline via Jupyter Notebook, identifying the real business factors out of transaction history data, and delivering it in such a managerially useful way that could be adopted at the executive level. The pipeline includes the processing from original files through the loading to the stage of explanatory graphs, while the reasoning for any analytical step is explained.

1.2 Who the Analysis Is For¶

The intended audience consists of the Sales and Marketing directors. They are not data scientists. The analysis focuses on four key business questions. First, the analysis examines which customers contribute most to revenue and the extent of customer concentration. Second, when exactly does demand go up and down across the year and across the week, because getting that wrong means either overstocking or running out at the worst time. Third, which countries outside the UK are actually worth spending marketing money on versus which ones just generate noise. Fourth, the analysis examines whether frequently co-purchased products could provide opportunities for bundling and cross-selling. Each chart is designed to address one of these four business questions directly, following the principle that a good visualisation should make the viewer think about the data rather than the design.

1.3 About the Dataset¶

The analysis uses the UCI Online Retail dataset, originally donated to the UCI Machine Learning Repository and available on Kaggle at the URL listed on the title page. It has 541,909 rows covering transactions from 1 December 2010 to 9 December 2011. Each row is one product line on one invoice, with eight columns: InvoiceNo, StockCode, Description, Quantity, InvoiceDate, UnitPrice, CustomerID, and Country. The dataset was selected for three main reasons. The dataset was not used in the module exercises and therefore meets the brief's requirement for a new dataset. It is large enough and messy enough to require real cleaning decisions, not just a quick dropna call. And its structure supports several genuinely different analytical angles, from customer segmentation to seasonal patterns to market-level comparisons.

2. Data Exploration - Characteristics & Quality Issues¶

2.1 Data Quality Assessment¶

Before conducting the main analysis, standard data-profiling checks were performed. The dataset contained 541,909 rows and 8 columns. The isna().sum() function was applied to every column, while describe() was used to examine the two numeric columns, Quantity and UnitPrice. Exact duplicates were identified using duplicated().sum(), and the number of rows containing non-positive Quantity or UnitPrice was explicitly calculated. InvoiceDate was also checked for unparseable entries, and the date range was examined to ensure that the temporal coverage was sensible. These checks are important because conducting visual analysis without first assessing data quality can result in misleading findings (Guo et al., 2023).

2.2 Quality Assessment Results¶

Seven data-quality issues emerged from the profiling. Each issue is considered because the cleaning decisions in Section 3 follow directly from the profiling results. Missing CustomerID affected roughly a quarter of all rows. This represents a substantial proportion of the records. These records still contained valid quantities, prices, dates and countries and may therefore represent genuine transaction records (Sharifnia et al., 2026). Excluding these records from all analyses would remove a substantial number of transaction records and reduce the coverage of transaction-level analysis (Dzuranin, 2022). Those records can be kept for transaction-level analyses but should be dropped from customer identification-based analyses. Missing Description affected less than one percent of the records. Although the proportion was small, these records were removed because product descriptions were required for reliable product-level interpretation and visualisation. All in all, the team was able to pinpoint 5,268 perfect duplicate rows where every single column had exactly the same values, leaving no room for doubts. Yet, they were not able to pin down the exact reason for such duplication just data shows. Retaining these records could result in double-counting of transaction lines, so the duplicates were removed before further analysis (Alotaibi, Pardede and Tomy, 2023). Negative and zero Quantity appeared in 10,624 rows, while negative or zero UnitPrice occurred in another 2,517 rows. Cross-checking against InvoiceNo revealed that the vast majority of negative-quantity rows had invoice numbers beginning with C, indicating cancellations. A small number of records with negative prices but no C prefix represented non-sales adjustments. These records were not positive-value sales transactions and could distort revenue-based analysis, so they were excluded from the sales-focused dataset (Souza, 2026). There were no unparseable values in the InvoiceDate columns and the time period covered by the dataset went from December 2010 to December 2011. The dataset was continuously dated with no skipped periods or suspicious entries. The column was subsequently decomposed into Month, DayOfWeek and Hour for the time-based analyses. Non-product StockCodes such as POST, D, M, BANK CHARGES, DOT, C2 and PADS represent postage fees, discounts or manual adjustments rather than merchandise. These records were therefore excluded because retaining them could cause non-merchandise entries to appear in product-level rankings and distort the interpretation of best-selling products.

3. Data Preprocessing¶

Each cleaning decision below is tied directly to an issue found above, not applied blindly:

  • Exact duplicate rows dropped - otherwise they double-count quantity and revenue.
  • Cancellations (InvoiceNo starts with 'C') split into a separate cancelled frame, not deleted - needed later for returns analysis.
  • Non-product StockCodes removed from the sales frame (fees, not merchandise).
  • Rows with Quantity <= 0 or UnitPrice <= 0 removed from clean - adjustments/errors already captured separately, not genuine sales.
  • CustomerID missing values are kept in clean (used for revenue/time/country insights), dropped only inside the two customer-level insights, so 25% of revenue isn't discarded unnecessarily.
  • TotalPrice = Quantity x UnitPrice added as the revenue unit of analysis.
  • InvoiceDate decomposed into Month, DayOfWeek, Hour for the time-pattern insights.

3.1 Data Cleaning Procedures and Rationale¶

Each cleaning step was directly linked to an identified data-quality issue. This approach makes the data-cleaning process explicit, reproducible and justified rather than applying a generic cleaning procedure. First, the 5,268 duplicate rows were removed. Second, cancellation invoices with InvoiceNo beginning with C were separated into a dataframe named cancelled and retained for the cancellation-rate analysis in Section 4.6. Third, non-product StockCodes were removed. Fourth, remaining rows with zero or negative Quantity or UnitPrice were excluded. Fifth, rows with missing CustomerID or Description were removed to create the clean customer-level dataset. Sixth, a TotalPrice column was created by multiplying Quantity by UnitPrice to provide the main transaction-level revenue measure. Finally, InvoiceDate was decomposed into Month, DayOfWeek and Hour for the time-based analyses. After that processes had been completed on the dataset, the cleaned dataframe consisted of 391,150 rows with no missing values and total revenue of GBP 8 737 228. The frame with cancelled records comprised 9,251 rows. A further 136,240 entries were discarded mainly because they were non-product related, contained invalid or blank data, had no customer identifiers. Summing up the three values we arrived at the grand total of 536,641 which was equal to the post-deduplication quantity exactly. Such a financial audit verification was a key to showing that the cleaning operations did not cause any rows to disappear mysteriously (Bousdekis et al., 2023).

4. Explanatory Data Visualization - Key Insights¶

4.1 Revenue Concentration Among Customers (the 80/20 Risk)¶

Why this matters: If the business depends heavily on a small number of customers, then losing just a few of them is not a minor setback. It is a serious financial hit. The leadership team needs to know how steep that concentration is before deciding how much to invest in key-account management (Ogrizović, Drašković and Bojić, 2024). By assigning customer ranks based on total spending, the authors drew a cumulative revenue curve. The results demonstrate a large share of the revenue is captured by the customer segment with the highest average spending per customer. 20 percent of the highest customers, about 867 of the 4,334 surveyed, accounted for 75 percent of the total income. The findings indicate that income is greatly centered around the highest- spending customers (John, Shobayo and Ogunleye, 2023). A loss of about one dozen customers at that level will be more severe than a hundred from the lower spending segment.

What the analysis recommends: establish a key-account programme for the highest-value customer group rather than distributing retention resources evenly.

4.2 Customer Segmentation via RFM (Recency, Frequency, Monetary)¶

Why this matters: Knowing that revenue is concentrated helps you understand that most of your revenue comes from a few big customers. But, it doesn't identify which of those big customers are becoming less and less loyal or are losing interest in your business altogether. Your customers may be categorized based on Recency (how recently they bought a product), Frequency (how often they bought products), and Monetary (how much they spent). RFM analysis helps identify different purchasing behaviours and customer value profiles (Ho et al., 2023; Arefin et al., 2024).

The customers were rated separately for Recency, Frequency, and Monetary value. Their scores from these categories were aggregated and customers were then grouped into Champions Loyal At Risk (high-value customer), and Hibernating/Low value categories. The At Risk group represents an important segment. These 305 customers had historically spent well but had not ordered recently. This segment represents a potential retention risk because these customers have historically generated relatively high value but have not purchased recently (Han and Kwak, 2023). Without further investigation or targeted retention activity, some of this customer value may be lost. Champions, on the other hand, are already buying regularly and do not need aggressive outreach. They need loyalty perks.

In [8]:
snapshot = clean['InvoiceDate'].max() + pd.Timedelta(days=1)
rfm = clean.dropna(subset=['CustomerID']).groupby('CustomerID').agg(
    Recency=('InvoiceDate', lambda x: (snapshot - x.max()).days),
    Frequency=('InvoiceNo', 'nunique'),
    Monetary=('TotalPrice', 'sum'))

r_score = pd.qcut(rfm['Recency'], 4, labels=[4,3,2,1]).astype(int)
f_score = pd.qcut(rfm['Frequency'].rank(method='first'), 4, labels=[1,2,3,4]).astype(int)
m_score = pd.qcut(rfm['Monetary'], 4, labels=[1,2,3,4]).astype(int)
rfm['RFM_Score'] = r_score + f_score + m_score

def segment(row):
    if row['RFM_Score'] >= 10: return 'Champions'
    if row['RFM_Score'] >= 8: return 'Loyal'
    if r_score[row.name] <= 2 and m_score[row.name] >= 3: return 'At Risk (high value)'
    return 'Hibernating / Low value'

rfm['Segment'] = rfm.apply(segment, axis=1)
seg_summary = rfm.groupby('Segment').agg(Customers=('Recency','size'), Revenue=('Monetary','sum')).sort_values('Revenue', ascending=False)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7,4.5))
bars = ax.barh(seg_summary.index, seg_summary['Revenue']/1000, color='#2ca02c')
ax.set_xlabel('Total historic revenue (GBP  thousands)')
ax.set_title('Revenue by Customer Segment (RFM)')
for b, n in zip(bars, seg_summary['Customers']):
    ax.text(b.get_width()+5, b.get_y()+b.get_height()/2, f'{n} customers', va='center', fontsize=9)

4.3 Monthly Seasonality¶

Why this matters: Gift-ware demand can vary across the year, making the identification of temporal sales patterns relevant for planning and forecasting. However, the timing of peaks should be established from observed transaction data rather than assumed in advance (Kolassa, 2022). Accurate identification of the timing of increased demand can support more effective inventory and staffing planning and reduce the risk of under- or over-preparation. Revenue was aggregated by month and plotted against the annual average using a dashed reference line. For most of the year, monthly revenue sits in a fairly narrow band. A clear increase begins in September, followed by further growth in October and a peak in November. Revenue increased from September through October, with November representing the highest monthly revenue in the observed period. This pattern matches well with the idea of people needing their supplies more right before Christmas but of course the transaction data weren't totally sufficient to determine the real cause of the increase. Decline in December 2011 is very drastic, but it is a kind of a trick: only 9 December is contained in the dataset, the major part of the month is missing.

4.4 When Do Customers Actually Order? (Day x Hour Heatmap)¶

Why this matters: Scheduling customer support and timing marketing emails are both decisions that can be informed by observed customer ordering patterns rather than relying on a default nine-to-five assumption (Anwar et al., 2025). To analyze unique order counts by day of the week and hour of the day, a heat map was built. The heat map delivers the message that orders are mostly placed Tuesday to Thursday, in the time range of 10 am to 3 pm, Wednesday at noon being the only really busy time slot. The weekend pattern is also notable. Saturday is completely blank. Zero orders. Not low activity, literally zero. Sunday, on the other hand, still sees a decent volume, around 60 percent of a typical weekday (Hadjimichael, Schlumberger and Haasnoot, 2024). The absence of Saturday orders may reflect business operating practices, system availability or another factor that cannot be established from the transaction data alone. The pattern therefore warrants further investigation.

4.5 Revenue Outside the UK - Where Is the Growth Opportunity?¶

Why this matters: The client is UK-registered, but orders come in from dozens of countries. Knowing which markets generate substantial purchasing activity, versus those producing relatively limited orders, can help inform decisions about customer behaviour and market-level commercial activity. Revenue was aggregated by country, with the UK separated from non-UK markets. The UK takes about 83 percent of total revenue. It is rather natural if the retailer happens to be UK-based. Even so, the second most fascinating fact is that the countries just below the UK are very important and among them, the major one is the Netherlands and Ireland. Germany, France and Australia also contribute their share, all of which are above the noise level (Eberhard, 2023). The combined revenue contribution from these five countries is not insignificant. In essence, there has already been sufficient demand, meaning these potential countries could be good targets for carrying out different local marketing tests.

4.6 Where Are Returns/Cancellations Concentrated?¶

Why this matters: A country may generate substantial revenue while also showing a relatively high cancellation rate. High cancellation levels can therefore identify markets requiring further investigation into transaction, fulfilment or customer-service processes. The cancellation rate for each country was calculated as the absolute value of cancelled revenue divided by gross sales, using a minimum gross sales threshold of GBP 5,000 to reduce the effect of very small denominators. The UK baseline is around 9 percent and provides a reference point. Several smaller markets showed cancellation rates substantially above this reference level. A rate above 100 percent means that the cancelled value exceeded the gross sales value recorded for that country during the period. This can occur when large cancelled transactions are recorded relative to modest new sales. Such results should be treated as signals for further investigation rather than direct evidence of a fulfilment problem.

4.7 Which Products Are Bought Together? (Cross-Sell Opportunity)¶

Why this matters: When customers purchase several products concurrently, the seller/retailer has the option of bundling them up/ offering a combined deal, showing the products adjacent to each other on the site, and triggering auto-suggestions when a particular good/basket gets selected. One very economical method of boosting the customers spending is this one because there is no requirement of attracting new consumers. The 30 most frequently purchased products were selected, grouped by invoice, and analysed by counting product pairs occurring within the same invoice. Several product pairs showed high co-occurrence within the same invoices. Repeated purchasing combinations can be useful for identifying potential product bundling and cross-selling opportunities. Many of the identified pairs consist of variants within the same product range, such as bags with matching designs or tableware from related themed collections (Mavrogiorgos et al., 2022). The recurring combinations suggest that related products may be purchased together. These observed associations provide a useful starting point for testing product bundles and cross-selling recommendations, although further testing would be required to establish their commercial impact.

5. Discussion and Conclusion¶

5.1 What Worked Well¶

All seven insights come from the same cleaned transaction dataset, and every cleaning step is documented and verified. This is a step towards reproducibility because the documented pipeline can be followed and verified using the same data and procedures. The insights themselves cover four business parts: who the customers are and how to retain them, when demand peaks, where international growth might come from, and what products to bundle. That breadth is intentional. A report that only answers one question, though well, is not that useful to a leadership team that has to take decisions across multiple functions.

5.2 What Could Be Better¶

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Dropping the rows without CustomerID meant losing about 25 percent of transactions from the customer-level analyses. The segmentation and Pareto findings are based only on transactions with identifiable CustomerIDs; therefore, the results may not fully represent customer activity associated with unidentified transactions. The dataset covers only one year, so the seasonality pattern is a single cycle. The seasonal pattern may recur in subsequent years; however, this cannot be established from a single year of transaction data. Cross-sell analysis was only for the top 30 products. So, a combination that might be great was left out, because of the low popularity of that product. As far as cancellation-rate numbers for low-volume countries are concerned, they are naturally quite variable, e. g. a cancellation of one large order can much alter the cancellation rate.

5.3 Recommendations¶

Pulling together the individual recommendations from each insight, the following priorities are proposed for the client:

  1. Set up a key-account program for the top 20 percent of customers who drive about 75 percent of revenue.
  2. Run a win-back campaign aimed at the 305 At Risk customers before their revenue disappears completely.
  3. Get stock and staffing locked in by August to handle the September-November demand spike.
  4. Time marketing emails for Tuesday to Thursday mornings, and investigate why Saturday has zero order activity.
  5. Test localised marketing in the Netherlands, Ireland, Germany, France, and Australia before spreading budget more widely.
  6. Check fulfilment processes in countries with high cancellation rates before spending marketing money there.
  7. Bundle the product pairs that customers already buy together rather than guessing at combinations.

5.4 Final Thoughts¶

The project demonstrates that a single transactional dataset, when appropriately cleaned and visualised, can provide evidence for a wide range of business questions. None of the seven insights required machine learning or complex modelling. They were derived primarily through aggregation, counting and visualisation rather than complex predictive modelling. The value was not in the sophistication of the methods but in asking the right questions and presenting the answers in a way that connects to real decisions.
